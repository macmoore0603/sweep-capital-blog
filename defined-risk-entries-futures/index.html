Defined Risk Entries in Futures Trading: The Complete Framework

Sweep Capital Group • July 2026
Last updated: July 2026

If there's one concept that separates professional futures traders from retail, it's this:

Every trade has defined risk before entry.

Not "I'll see how it goes." Not "I'll move my stop if it goes against me." Not "I'll average down if it dips."

Defined risk means: before you click buy or sell, you know exactly where your stop goes, how many points you're risking, what dollar amount that represents, and what your maximum daily loss is if the trade fails.

This article breaks down the defined risk framework for ES and NQ futures — the exact rules we teach at Sweep Capital Group.

What Defined Risk Is NOT

Defined risk is not a mental stop. A mental stop is no stop. If you're using mental stops, you're trading undefined risk — which means your risk is theoretically unlimited on every trade.

Defined risk is not a wide stop "to give it room." A stop that's too wide is the same as no stop — you're risking too much per trade to make the math work.

And defined risk is not the same as a fixed dollar stop. Your stop should be at the invalidation level — where the setup is wrong. You size the position so that the dollar loss at that level is within your 1-2% risk budget.

What Defined Risk Actually Is

Every trade has three numbers defined before entry:

  • Invalidation level — the price at which the setup is wrong (where your stop goes)
  • Position size — calculated so that hitting the stop costs you no more than 1-2% of your account
  • Daily loss limit — the total daily drawdown at which you stop trading for the day

These three numbers are non-negotiable. You commit to them before the trade. You don't move them during the trade.

The Math: Position Sizing for ES Futures

ES futures have these specifications:

  • Tick size: 0.25 points (4 ticks per point)
  • Tick value: $12.50 per tick ($50 per point)
  • 1 point on ES = $50 per contract

The Formula

Position size (contracts) = (Account × Risk%) ÷ (Stop distance in points × $50)

Example 1: $10,000 Account, 1% Risk, 5-Point Stop

  • Risk amount: $10,000 × 1% = $100
  • Stop distance: 5 points
  • Cost per contract: 5 × $50 = $250
  • Position size: $100 ÷ $250 = 0.4 contracts

Result: You can't take this trade with 1 contract. The risk per contract ($250) exceeds your $100 risk budget. You either:

  • Skip the trade (stop is too wide for your account)
  • Wait for a setup with a tighter stop (3-4 points)

Example 2: $10,000 Account, 1% Risk, 2-Point Stop

  • Risk amount: $100
  • Stop distance: 2 points
  • Cost per contract: 2 × $50 = $100
  • Position size: $100 ÷ $100 = 1 contract

Result: 1 contract with a 2-point stop risks exactly $100 — 1% of a $10,000 account. This is a valid setup.

Example 3: $25,000 Account, 1.5% Risk, 4-Point Stop

  • Risk amount: $375
  • Cost per contract: 4 × $50 = $200
  • Position size: $375 ÷ $200 = 1.875 → 1 contract

Result: 1 contract risks $200, which is 0.8% of the account — within the 1.5% budget. You don't round up for futures. Always round down.

The 3-Layer Risk Framework

Layer 1: Per-Trade Risk (1-2%)

Every trade risks max 1-2% of the account. No exceptions. This is calculated before entry using the formula above.

Layer 2: Daily Loss Limit (3%)

After losing 3% of the account in a single day, you stop trading. Not "one more trade." Not "I'll get it back." You're done for the day.

If you have a $10,000 account, your daily loss limit is $300. That's 3 losing trades at 1% each — or 1.5 losing trades at 2% each. When you hit $300 in losses, close the platform.

Layer 3: Weekly Drawdown Limit (6%)

After losing 6% of the account in a week, you stop trading for the rest of the week. This prevents the death spiral — where one bad day turns into a revenge trading week that blows the account.

$10,000 account → $600 weekly drawdown limit. Once you're down $600 on the week, you're done until Monday.

ICT Stop Placement: Where the Invalidation Lives

In the ICT/SMC framework, the stop goes at the sweep extreme — the point beyond which the setup is definitively wrong.

For a Session Sweep long:

  • Sweep candle took the session low and reversed
  • Stop goes 1-2 ticks below the sweep candle's wick
  • If price moves below the sweep, the setup was wrong — the "sweep" was actually a breakout

For a 5-15 gap retest entry:

  • Stop goes on the other side of the displacement candle
  • If price closes beyond the displacement, the gap was filled and the setup is invalid

The key principle: the stop is at the invalidation level, not at an arbitrary dollar amount. You find where the setup is wrong first, then size the position to fit your risk budget.

Common Failure Mode: Moving the Stop

The #1 way traders violate defined risk is moving the stop.

  • Price approaching the stop → trader moves it 2 ticks further "to give it room"
  • Price still approaching → trader moves it again
  • Eventually, the stop is so far away that one loss takes out 10% of the account

If the stop is hit, the setup was wrong. Taking a small loss is the cost of trading. Moving the stop converts a small loss into a catastrophic one.

Rule: once the stop is placed, it does not move. If the trade hits the stop, take the loss.

A Real Example

Setup: Session Sweep long on ES
  • Sweep at 7450 (session low taken at 7447)
  • Displacement candle closes back at 7452
  • Entry: 7452 (on retest)
  • Stop: 7446 (1 tick below sweep wick)
  • Risk: 6 points × $50 = $300 per contract
  • Account: $20,000
  • Position size: $300 ÷ $300 = 1 contract (exactly 1.5% risk)
  • Target: 7465 (session midpoint)
  • Reward: 13 points × $50 = $650
  • R:R = 1:2.17
  • Result: target hit, +$650

Everything was defined before entry. If the trade had failed, the loss was $300 — 1.5% of the account. The next trade would have been taken with the same discipline.
